# Sources and adaptation notes

6 September 2026 · Accompanies the first complete abridged story draft

## Text and provenance

The primary text for this project is the ancient Sanskrit in `dist/verses.json`, whose provenance and source revision are recorded in the repository's `SOURCES.md`. It was extracted from the [gita/gita verse dataset](https://github.com/gita/gita/blob/main/data/verse.json) with modern translations and commentary removed. The new script revises the existing project's original English adaptation, `dist/chapters.js`, into a continuous dramatic telling.

All spoken English in the new script is newly authored adaptation. No modern translation is reproduced as the project's dialogue. References are passage anchors, not a claim that each script paragraph translates every word of its cited range. The entire scripture remains distinct from the abridged experience.

External source checks for this draft used the ancient text and translations accessible at IIT Kanpur's Gita Supersite, and K. M. Ganguli's public-domain English translation of the Mahabharata. These are aids to checking context and sequence; one translator's interpretation is not presented as the only Hindu understanding.

The draft has received an internal editorial pass, not independent Sanskrit or scholarly review. There is no claim of historical reconstruction for the artwork, staging, costume or duration of the encounter.

## Optional background copy

**Who is Arjun?** Arjun—Arjuna in Sanskrit—is one of the five Pandava brothers. An accomplished archer, he enters the scene with the skills to fight but becomes unsure whether fighting his own relatives and teachers can be right. The opening chapter supplies this conflict without requiring the reader to know his earlier exploits.

**Why is Krishna driving?** Before the war, Arjun chose Krishna's presence even though Krishna had said he would not fight. Duryodhana chose Krishna's forces. Arjun asked Krishna to be his charioteer. This relationship gives the battlefield conversation its intimacy. [Udyoga Parva VII](https://sacred-texts.com/hin/m05/m05007.htm)

**Why did peace fail?** The cousins' dispute over rule had resisted reconciliation. Krishna recounts attempts to reach agreement, including a minimal territorial settlement, before the forces moved toward Kurukshetra. The main path needs only that background; a longer retelling of the dice game and exile is not required before Arjun speaks. [Udyoga Parva 150](https://sacred-texts.com/hin/m05/m05150.htm)

**Who are Bhishma and Drona?** Bhishma is a revered elder of the family; Drona is Arjun's teacher. In the scene they stand with the opposing force. That relationship is why the camera pauses on them. Their wider motives and obligations belong to the larger Mahabharata, rather than a simple division into good and bad people. Sources: Gita 1.24–27 and 2.4.

**Who is telling this?** Sanjaya reports to Dhritarashtra, father of the sons on the opposing side. In the conclusion Sanjaya attributes his hearing of the sacred conversation to Vyasa's grace. Our near view on the chariot is an imaginative viewpoint for the reader, not another person added to this narrative frame. Sources: 1.1–2 and 18.74–75.

## Verse numbering and source links

The local edition has 701 entries. It includes Arjun's opening question as 13.1 and therefore has 35 verses in chapter XIII. Editions that omit that question commonly total 700 verses and number the following chapter XIII verses one lower. ## Interpretation notes to retain

**1.40–44 — Family, gender and social order.** Arjun fears the loss of family rites and describes it through assumptions about women and varna that belong to the text's social world. His speech is an argument within the dialogue, not a neutral narrator declaring women responsible for society's moral condition. The short script retains his fear of family destruction and opens this context alongside it; the unabridged Sanskrit is not altered.

**2.19–30 — The enduring self.** Krishna's account of the self beyond bodily death is a metaphysical and religious teaching. Do not paraphrase it as "death is unreal, so injury does not matter." The later demands for non-harming, compassion and freedom from hostility must remain visible in the adaptation.

**2.31–38; 3.35; 18.41–48 — Duty.** Svadharma concerns one's own duty or responsibility in the text's moral and social setting. Arjun's duty as a warrior is integral to this encounter. A reader's modern dilemma can illuminate a part of the teaching without being declared equivalent to this war.

**4.13; 9.32; 18.41–48 — Social hierarchy.** The text names varna, differentiated duties, and language of birth and gender. Verse 9.32 includes the difficult term papa-yonayah, rendered in different ways around disfavoured or sinful birth; translations also differ in how they relate it to the groups named afterward. Its invitation to spiritual refuge does not make the surrounding hierarchy disappear. Neither claim that the Gita is simply a modern equal-opportunity manifesto nor use the adaptation to rank people's worth by birth. Readers should be able to inspect the text and multiple commentaries. This draft compresses the social prescriptions; it does not claim to have resolved their interpretation.

**6.40–45; 8; 15.7–11 — Rebirth and liberation.** Preserve these as part of the teaching. An analogy about habits, attention or changing circumstances is supplementary, not an alternative translation of rebirth. Moksha is not merely confidence, productivity or lower stress.

**9.22; 9.30–31 — Care and transformation.** Divine care is not a guarantee of prosperity or immunity from suffering. A turn toward devotion is not permission to continue wrongdoing without repair or changed conduct.

**11.8; 11.32; 11.45–51 — The vision.** Krishna grants sight; the reader does not unlock it by passing a quiz. Kala at 11.32 is rendered as Time here, including its destructive force. The request for a crowned, four-armed form and the return to a gentle human form give our visual transition its anchors. Details of how the vision unfolds remain interpretive.

**13 and 15 — Self and supreme reality.** Keep the text's distinctions among field, knower, nature, conscious principle and Krishna. Avoid claiming that every tradition explains their relationships identically. Brahman and the creator deity Brahma are different terms.

**14 and 17 — Gunas.** These are qualities of nature within a spiritual and cosmological teaching, not medical categories. The food discussion in XVII is not modern dietary guidance. The treatment must not map sattva, rajas or tamas onto skin colour or a social group's supposedly fixed worth.

**16 — Divine and demonic qualities.** The adaptation emphasises conduct and dispositions. It does not add contemporary enemy groups to the text's categories.

**18.63–73 — Choice and refuge.** Keep the sequence intact. The invitation to consider and choose is followed by Krishna's further words, the invitation to surrender in 18.66, and Arjun's answer in 18.73. The meaning of relinquishing all dharmas is interpreted differently across commentarial traditions. This first adaptation retains the phrase with context instead of claiming it settles the question of free will or cancels ethical responsibility.

## What is ours

The silent witness position, low sunlight, faceless figures, hand gestures, meaningful pauses, the matching opening and closing view of the bow, and all movement through teaching landscapes are authored staging. The physical chariot remains between the armies through the conversation. We do not assert that eighteen chapters required a journey across eighteen places, that time literally stopped, or that this artwork reproduces Arjun's divine sight.

The literary chapter titles, three-movement structure, optional reflection questions and modern-facing explanations are editorial framing. Sanskrit chapter designations and complete verse access belong to the source layer. A permanent but unobtrusive adaptation label should accompany the finished reader.
